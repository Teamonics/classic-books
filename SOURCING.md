# Classic Books — Sourcing Decisions

Date: 2026-08-15. Every claim below was verified today against the named
source (SE = Standard Ebooks released catalog, PG = Project Gutenberg ebook
number, WS = English Wikisource, MIA = marxists.org). Companion to PLAN.md.

## Governing rules (from you, 2026-08-15)

1. Content first: best public-domain versions, easily downloadable.
2. No source or any copyright concern ⇒ **leave the book out for now**.
3. Prefer the translations used in the Franklin Library / Britannica Great
   Books set **wherever possible** (referred to below as "the 1952 set" —
   per the legal constraint we never use that trademarked name in the
   product, its copy, metadata, or URLs).

### The Fitzgerald Iliad is not possible

Robert Fitzgerald's Iliad was published in 1974 and he died in 1985: it is
copyrighted in the US into the 2060s and in life+70 countries until 2056.
Same for Lattimore, Fagles, Emily Wilson — every modern verse Homer is
decades away. Applying rule 2 and rule 3: the 1952 set used **Samuel
Butler's prose Homer**, which is PD everywhere and on PG — that is what's
sourced below. (PD verse alternatives if you ever want one: Bryant on SE,
Pope, Cowper.)

### A note on rule 3 and the law

Following the 1952 set's *translation choices* is defensible: these are
almost all standard Victorian PD translations, we adopt none of their volume
groupings or arrangement (we order chronologically), several titles diverge
(below), and we never use the trademark. Two of their picks are themselves
copyright traps we must NOT follow: their Thucydides is Crawley **revised by
Feetham** and their Summa is the Dominican text **revised by Sullivan**, and
their Capital is Moore-Aveling **revised by Sachey & Lamm** — the revisions
carry 1952-era copyrights. We use the unrevised bases in all three cases
(which the project brief already required). Their Chaucer (Nicolson/Krapp
modernizations, 1932–34) is still US-copyrighted until 2028–2030 — we keep
Skeat's Middle English.

## OUT for now (rule 2 applied)

| Work | Reason |
|---|---|
| **Euripides (all 19 plays)** | No single public-domain translation exists as complete clean digital text. Verified 2026-08-15: Coleridge (the 1952 set's pick, d. 1936) has only **10** plays transcribed on Wikisource; Way (d. 1930, clear since 2001) has **16**; their union covers 18, leaving *Cyclops*, for which Shelley's translation exists separately. Assembling all 19 therefore means (a) a Wikisource adapter for proofread-page markup, and (b) mixing three translators within one author, recorded per play. Plan below; not yet built. |
| **Plotinus, Enneads** | Their MacKenna & **Page** text: Page was alive in 1969 (revising the 4th ed.), so life+70 runs into the 2040s+. No PD-everywhere complete alternative exists. |
| **Rousseau** | Cole (their pick and SE's) d. 1959 → protected until 2030. Tozer alternative exists only as page scans. Revisit 2030 or when a clean Tozer text appears. |
| **Montesquieu, Spirit of Laws** | Nugent translation is PD but not on PG/WS as clean text — scan-only. Revisit. |
| **Pascal, Pensées** | PG #18269 carries no translator attribution in the file; it is believed to be W. F. Trotter (their pick) but his death date is unverified. Do not ship until attribution + death date are confirmed. |
| Aristotle, Hegel, Goethe, Boswell | Already deferred in the brief (Ross EU-protected; Priest's Faust 1941 is US-protected to 2037; substitutes are later work). |

## Euripides plan (when built)

Preferred order per play, so the Franklin/1952 choice wins where it exists:

1. **Coleridge** (prose, d. 1936) — Alcestis, Helen, Heracleidae, Hippolytus,
   Ion, Medea, Rhesus, The Bacchantes, The Suppliants, The Trojan Women.
2. **Way** (verse, d. 1930) — Andromache, Electra, Hecuba, Iphigeneia at
   Aulis, Iphigeneia in Taurica, Orestes, The Madness of Herakles, The
   Phoenician Maidens.
3. **Shelley** (d. 1822) — Cyclops.

All three are public domain in the US and in life+70 jurisdictions. Each
play's translator is recorded individually in sources.json and shown on the
colophon, because the set is not by one hand.
